## 2. Framework grid

### Performance
**Reported unlisted returns keep outrunning distributed cash, and appraisal smoothing is increasingly named as the reason.**
- Evidence: distributions-to-paid-in (DPI) has been stuck near 14–15% of NAV since late 2022; one model projects only a modest improvement toward the high-teens for 2026, with liquidity normalisation "structurally incomplete." Liquidity is increasingly synthetic — NAV financing, preferred equity, continuation vehicles and recapitalisations rather than true realisations. 2025 exit value reached $905bn, but 78% was concentrated in mega-exits; mid-market inventory is effectively stagnant, part of a reported ~34,000 unsold PE-backed companies overhang. Cliffwater Direct Lending Index: 2025 return of 9.3% against 10.4% interest income plus 0.7pp of PIK interest — realised return running below headline income.
- Implication: discount reported unlisted IRR/TVPI until DPI confirms it; treat CV- and NAV-loan-funded "distributions" as leverage-assisted liquidity engineering, not organic realisation.
- Watch: Q3 DPI commentary from large LPs and at Q3 manager earnings calls; whether mid-market exit value share recovers from the current mega-deal concentration.
- Counter-case: the largest platforms are producing genuinely accelerating cash performance — Blackstone net realisations +27% YoY and gross performance revenue +32% YoY this quarter; Apollo, TPG and Carlyle all beat distributable-earnings estimates. The drought reads as concentrated in mid-market/older vintages, not universal.

### Valuations
**Buyout and credit secondaries are pricing close enough to NAV that the traditional discount has thinned to a few points; real estate remains the outlier discount.**
- Evidence (Jefferies, full-year 2025 benchmark): buyout secondaries priced at 92% of NAV, credit at 91%, venture/growth at 78%, real estate at 70%; average LP portfolio pricing 87% of NAV. 2025 global secondary volume hit a record $240bn (+48% YoY); H1 2026 volume alone is expected to exceed $100bn on backlog, with a path toward $300bn/year over 12–24 months. Infrastructure secondaries — a near-niche segment — could reach $30bn in 2026. Clearing-price evidence from live deals: EA's record $55bn LBO and Apollo's premium-priced win over Castlelake for EasyJet both show PE bidders still paying up for scaled, cash-generative assets.
- Implication: the secondaries discount for buyout and credit (8–9 points off NAV) is thin enough that it is closer to a transaction-cost spread than a genuine illiquidity discount; real estate (30 points off NAV) is where carrying values still look stale versus clearing prices.
- Watch: whether real-estate secondaries pricing converges toward the 90% band as ODCE redemption queues clear (queues down to ~12% of NAV from a ~20% peak, some funds fully cleared); whether infrastructure secondaries pricing holds as volume scales 3x+.
- Counter-case: the discount rate (long end) is still the standing valuation input and has not fallen enough to mechanically re-rate cap rates down — net lease cap rates actually ticked up 2–10bps in Q2 2026 (overall to 6.82%, industrial to 7.25%) even as H1 2026 CRE transaction volume rose 31% YoY to $293bn — deal volume is recovering faster than cap rates are compressing, i.e. marks are arguably still catching down to a higher-for-longer discount rate rather than being bid through.

### Leverage
**Stress is broadening beneath the AUM headlines — PIK share and non-accruals are both climbing at the loan level even as fund-level dry powder and fundraising metrics look pristine.**
- Evidence: Fitch put the private-credit default rate at a record ~6% through Q2 2026. A Boston Fed study (published Aug 2026) found BDC loans' PIK share of income rose from ~5.4% in Q1 2022 to 9.8% in Q1 2026 (peaking ~9.85% in Q4 2025) — the co-author called it "a sign of stress." BDC non-accruals are rising and dispersed: Crescent 5.7% of cost, Monroe 4.0%, FSK 4.2% (with FSK's NAV down 9.9% over the same stretch and 4, 5 then 7 new non-accrual names added in successive recent quarters — the rate of new impairment is accelerating, not just the level); OBDC and ARCC remain comparatively low, in the low single digits. NAV lending has gone "mainstream" — typical LTVs of 10–25% of portfolio value, pricing 4–7% over the benchmark rate (Rede Partners, 2026) — a structural leverage layer stacked on top of underlying fund/portfolio-company leverage, increasingly used to fund follow-ons and manufacture distributions.
- Implication: headline BDC/private-credit metrics (AUM, fundraising) look healthy in aggregate, but income quality is deteriorating at the loan level; manager dispersion (OBDC/ARCC vs. FSK/Crescent) matters more than the index average, and PIK-funded "yield" plus NAV-loan-funded "distributions" both substitute non-cash or borrowed liquidity for real cash generation.
- Watch: Q2 2026 BDC 10-Qs (filing through August) for PIK ratios crossing the 10% threshold the Boston Fed flagged; NAV-loan usage disclosure at continuation-vehicle closings.
- Counter-case: OBDC and ARCC continue to post low single-digit non-accrual rates, and record dry powder/fundraising at KKR ($143bn), Ares ($170bn) and Blackstone show no sign that lenders are capital-constrained — this currently reads as idiosyncratic manager/vintage dispersion rather than a systemic private-credit leverage problem.

### Dry powder
**Dry powder and fundraising keep hitting records at the largest platforms even as the marginal retail buyer turns more price-sensitive, not less.**
- Evidence: record fundraising this quarter — Ares $36bn, Brookfield $77bn (PE/infra/credit-led), KKR $34bn (LTM record $133bn), TPG $16bn (YTD $26bn, targeting $50bn for 2026), Carlyle $30bn in H1 2026 (a firm record, achieved before its flagship funds were even fully in market, prompting management to call it entering a "fundraising super cycle"). Record dry powder: KKR $143bn, Ares $170bn; global private-market dry powder sits near $3.7trn overall (core PE/VC/debt/real assets ~$2.5trn), still rising ($195.8bn increase since end-2024) despite softer fundraising overall. KKR's largest-ever infrastructure fund close ($19.2bn, 3 Aug) took its infra platform toward $120bn — capital flowing specifically into AI/power-adjacent hard assets. The Nvidia ~$500bn AI-infrastructure financing consortium (Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, KKR) pulls five of the largest sponsors directly into funding AI datacentre buildout.
- Implication: dry powder is not sitting idle at the top of the market — it is actively bidding up AI-adjacent hard assets, exactly where a price-insensitive buyer would show up first.
- Watch: whether Nvidia-consortium-style vendor-financing structures show up in the same managers' Q3 leverage/non-accrual metrics; deployment pace against the $3.7trn dry-powder stock next quarter.
- Counter-case: nontraded BDC fundraising collapsed to just $2bn in Q2 2026, down 82% YoY — the retail/DC channel that is supposed to be the price-insensitive marginal buyer instead pulled back sharply, and mid-market/first-time-fund managers are reportedly facing "extended timelines, reduced targets, and increasingly skeptical LP investment committees." Dry powder is concentrating at mega-funds, not broadening across the market.

## 3. Standing question: is the illiquidity premium still being paid, or competed away?

**Bifurcated, not uniform.** At the mega-cap/AI-infrastructure end the premium looks close to competed away: buyout and credit secondaries price at 91–92% of NAV, fundraising and dry powder are at records across Blackstone/KKR/Ares/TPG/Brookfield, EA's $55bn LBO is the largest ever, Apollo outbid Castlelake for EasyJet, and five of the largest sponsors are now direct funders of Nvidia's ~$500bn AI-infrastructure programme. But the premium is still being paid — arguably widening — in the sleeves showing real stress: private-credit/BDC quality (record ~6% default rate, PIK income share near the 10% threshold the Boston Fed flagged as a stress signal, non-accruals up to 4–6% of cost at several BDCs and still rising), real-estate secondaries still clearing near 70% of NAV, and the retail/DC channel, which pulled back 82% YoY in nontraded BDC fundraising this quarter rather than chasing yield. Read: capital is being priced away at the scaled, AI-adjacent, cash-generative end of the market; it is not being priced away — and if anything credit risk is rising faster than compensation — in mid-market private credit and core real estate.
